## Before You Begin

Level 1 trained you to see.

You learned to look at the ordinary 
and ask uncomfortable questions. 
You practiced tracing individual experiences 
back to structural causes. 
You began to see power where 
you previously saw procedure, 
and history where you previously 
saw nature.

Now you need tools.

Seeing without tools is frustrating. 
You feel the wrongness of things 
but cannot explain it with precision. 
You sense that something invisible 
is producing the patterns you observe — 
but you cannot name it clearly enough 
to analyze it, write about it, 
or persuade anyone else to see it.

Level 2 gives you five tools. 
Built by five people over 150 years 
of sociological thinking.

---

### How to read this level

Each thinker is introduced as a human being 
before they are introduced as a theorist.

This is not an accident or a courtesy.

It is methodologically important.

Ideas do not arrive from nowhere. 
They are produced by specific people 
living through specific historical moments — 
asking questions that their circumstances 
made urgent. When you understand 
what a thinker was trying to solve, 
their concepts stop being abstract 
and become what they actually are: 
tools for seeing specific things 
in the world.

A hammer makes more sense 
when you know what nail it was built for.

Read each thinker in order. 
They build on each other. 
By the end of this level you will 
have five different lenses — 
and the beginning of the ability 
to use more than one at the same time.

That combination is where 
sociology becomes genuinely powerful.

## Thinker One — Karl Marx (1818–1883)

### Who he was

Karl Marx was born in the city of 
Trier, in what is now Germany, 
in 1818. His father was a lawyer — 
educated, relatively comfortable, 
a man of the Enlightenment who had 
converted from Judaism to Christianity 
to avoid the legal restrictions 
placed on Jewish professionals.

From the beginning, Marx's life 
was shaped by a contradiction 
he could not ignore: 
the gap between what his society 
promised — reason, progress, 
freedom — and what it actually 
delivered to most of the people 
living in it.

He went to university in Berlin 
and fell in with the Young Hegelians — 
a group of radical intellectuals 
who were trying to use philosophy 
to critique society rather than 
merely describe it. He was brilliant, 
argumentative, and almost immediately 
unemployable in any official institution 
because of his politics.

He spent most of his adult life 
in exile — eventually settling 
in London, the heart of the 
industrial world he was trying 
to understand. He lived in poverty 
that was not romantic or theoretical. 
His children died — several of them — 
partly because he could not afford 
adequate food, medicine, or heating. 
He buried three of his children 
in London. He was often unable 
to afford the stamps to mail 
his manuscripts to publishers.

He wrote *Das Kapital* — 
one of the most influential books 
in human history — in the reading 
rooms of the British Museum, 
surrounded by the evidence of 
what industrial capitalism was 
doing to the human beings 
who powered it.

He was not an armchair philosopher. 
He was a man watching people 
be destroyed by a system, 
trying to understand the machinery 
of that destruction precisely enough 
to explain why it was not an accident.

---

### The world he was watching

To understand Marx you must see 
what he saw.

The industrial revolution transformed 
England in the first half of the 
nineteenth century with a speed 
and violence that we have largely 
sanitized in retrospect.

Children as young as five worked 
in coal mines and textile factories — 
fourteen, sixteen hours a day, 
in conditions that destroyed 
their bodies before they were adults.

The average life expectancy 
of a factory worker in Manchester 
in the 1840s was around twenty-eight years.

Workers who were injured in factories 
received nothing — no compensation, 
no medical care, no alternative 
employment. They were replaced.

The people who owned the factories — 
the mills, the mines, the railways — 
were becoming spectacularly wealthy. 
They built grand houses. 
They funded universities and libraries. 
They served in parliament. 
They were celebrated as the 
engines of progress.

The people who worked in those 
factories were considered to be 
failing individually if they 
could not feed their families.

Marx watched this and asked 
a question that seems obvious 
only in retrospect:

> *What is the relationship between 
> the wealth of the owners 
> and the poverty of the workers? 
> Are these two separate facts — 
> or are they the same fact 
> seen from two different positions?*

His answer changed everything.

---

### His core tool — Class, capital, and the means of production

Marx's fundamental insight was this:

Every society is organized around 
the production of things people need — 
food, shelter, clothing, medicine, 
knowledge. The question of who controls 
the means of that production — 
the land, the factories, the technology, 
the capital — is the most important 
question in understanding how 
that society works and who it 
works for.

He called the people who own 
the means of production 
the **bourgeoisie** — the capitalist class.

He called the people who own 
nothing but their own labor — 
who must sell their time and bodies 
to survive — the **proletariat** — 
the working class.

These two groups are not 
merely economically different. 
They have structurally opposite interests. 
The owner's profit depends on 
paying workers as little as possible. 
The worker's survival depends on 
being paid as much as possible.

This is not a personal conflict. 
It is structural. 
It will persist regardless of 
whether individual owners are 
kind or cruel, generous or miserly — 
because it is built into the 
logic of the system itself.

---

**The concept of exploitation:**

Marx introduced a concept called 
**surplus value** — 
which is the gap between 
what workers produce 
and what they are paid.

If a worker produces goods 
worth one hundred dollars in a day, 
and is paid twenty dollars, 
the remaining eighty dollars 
is surplus value — 
extracted by the owner, 
accumulated as profit, 
reinvested as capital.

This is not illegal. 
This is not an aberration. 
This is how the system is designed to work.

Profit does not come from nowhere. 
It comes from the labor of workers 
who are paid less than the value 
they create.

Inequality is not a side effect 
of capitalism. 
It is a structural feature of it.

---

**The concept of ideology:**

Marx made one more contribution 
that is perhaps even more important 
for understanding the world today.

He asked: if workers are being 
exploited, why do they accept it? 
Why do they not always revolt?

His answer was the concept of **ideology** — 
the set of ideas, values, and beliefs 
that dominant classes promote 
to make the existing order feel 
natural, legitimate, and inevitable 
to everyone — including those it harms.

When a poor person believes 
their poverty is their own fault —
that is ideology.

When a patient believes the 
pharmaceutical company's pricing 
of essential medicine is just 
the market working properly — 
that is ideology.

When a worker believes that 
a billionaire "deserves" their wealth 
because they "worked hard" — 
as if the workers who built 
their company worked less hard — 
that is ideology.

Ideology does not require conspiracy. 
It is reproduced through culture, 
media, education, religion, 
and the accumulated common sense 
of a society organized to 
benefit those at the top.

---

### Applying Marx outside the West

It would be a mistake to think 
of Marx as only relevant to 
nineteenth-century European factories.

His tools are urgently relevant 
to the global present.

Consider the global pharmaceutical industry.

The intellectual property system 
that governs medicine is organized 
so that the companies who develop drugs — 
overwhelmingly based in wealthy countries, 
funded largely by public research money — 
retain the exclusive right to 
manufacture and price those drugs 
for decades.

The result: essential medicines 
for diseases that kill millions 
of people in low-income countries 
are priced far beyond what 
those countries' health systems 
can afford.

This is not an accident of the market. 
It is the deliberate design 
of a system in which the 
means of medical production — 
the research infrastructure, 
the intellectual property, 
the manufacturing capacity — 
are concentrated in the hands 
of a small number of corporations 
whose obligation is to 
their shareholders, not to 
the sick.

Marx would recognize this immediately. 
The form has changed. 
The structure is the same.

---

### Apply it to your own life — Step by step

**Step 1:**
Think about a product or service 
you use regularly. 
It might be medicine you take, 
food you eat, technology you use, 
healthcare you receive.

**Step 2:**
Ask the Marxist question: 
Who owns the means of producing this? 
Who profits from your use of it?

**Step 3:**
Ask: who produces it — 
who actually performs the labor? 
What are their conditions? 
What are they paid relative to 
what the product generates?

**Step 4:**
Ask: what ideology exists 
that makes the current ownership 
and profit structure feel natural 
or inevitable?

What would have to change 
for the means of producing this thing 
to be organized differently — 
in a way that served the users 
and the workers rather than 
primarily the owners?

You are now doing Marxist analysis. 
Not as a political position. 
As a sociological tool.

## Thinker Two — Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)

### Who he was

Émile Durkheim was born in Épinal, 
in northeastern France, 
the son and grandson of rabbis. 
His entire family assumed 
he would become a rabbi too.

He chose sociology instead — 
which in the 1880s was not 
a recognized academic discipline. 
He spent years fighting for 
its legitimacy, insisting that 
society could be studied 
with the same rigor as biology 
or physics, that social phenomena 
had causes that could be measured 
and analyzed.

He was methodical, exacting, 
almost obsessive in his desire 
to establish sociology as a science. 
He was also, beneath that precision, 
deeply troubled by what he saw 
happening to the society around him.

France in the late nineteenth century 
was convulsing through rapid 
industrial change, political crisis, 
the trauma of military defeat, 
and the erosion of the traditional 
communities — the village, the church, 
the guild — that had given people 
a sense of belonging and meaning 
for centuries.

Durkheim watched people becoming 
untethered. Lost. Isolated in ways 
that seemed to produce suffering 
that could not be explained 
by individual psychology.

He wanted to understand what 
holds societies together — 
and what happens when it breaks.

He found out personally. 
His only son André died 
in the Macedonian front in 1916, 
during the First World War. 
Durkheim himself died in 1917 — 
of what many described as grief 
as much as illness. 
He was fifty-nine.

---

### The world he was watching

Durkheim was writing at a time 
when the traditional structures 
of European society were dissolving.

For centuries, most people in Europe 
had lived in small communities — 
villages, parishes, craft guilds — 
where everyone knew everyone, 
where your role in the community 
was clear, where religious ritual 
gave collective meaning to 
birth, death, suffering, and celebration.

Industrialization destroyed much of this. 
People moved to cities. 
They worked in factories 
alongside strangers. 
The church lost authority. 
Traditional moral frameworks 
became uncertain.

And something strange happened: 
as people became materially wealthier 
on average, certain forms of 
suffering increased. 
Suicide rates rose. 
Social isolation deepened. 
People seemed to be gaining 
in individual freedom 
while losing something 
they could not name.

Durkheim named it.

---

### His core tool — Social facts, solidarity, and anomie

**Social facts:**

Durkheim's first and most fundamental 
contribution was the concept of 
**social facts.**

A social fact is any way of acting, 
thinking, or feeling that exists 
outside the individual — 
that has its own reality, 
independent of any particular person, 
and that exerts a constraining 
influence on the people within it.

Language is a social fact. 
You did not invent the language 
you think in. It existed before you. 
It will exist after you. 
It shapes what thoughts are 
even possible for you to form.

The suicide rate of a country 
is a social fact. 
It is not explained by adding up 
individual psychological crises. 
It is explained by the structure 
of the society — 
the degree of integration, 
the strength of collective meaning, 
the presence or absence of 
binding social ties.

This was Durkheim's most radical claim: 
that social phenomena have 
social causes — not just 
individual psychological ones — 
and must be studied at the 
social level to be understood.

---

**His study of suicide — 
and why it changed everything:**

In 1897, Durkheim published 
*Suicide: A Study in Sociology* — 
a book that transformed 
what sociology could do.

Suicide seems like the most 
personal act possible. 
The final private decision.

Durkheim showed that it has 
a social structure.

He analyzed suicide rates 
across different countries, 
different religions, different 
occupational groups, different 
levels of economic stability.

He found consistent patterns.

Protestants had higher suicide rates 
than Catholics. 
Not because of theological differences — 
but because Protestant communities 
were more individualistic, 
less tightly bound, 
with weaker social obligations 
between members.

Married people had lower suicide rates 
than unmarried people — 
not because marriage is psychologically 
protective in every case, 
but because integration 
into a binding social unit 
provides a form of connection 
to something beyond oneself.

Suicide rates rose during 
both economic crises 
and periods of sudden economic boom — 
because both disrupted 
the established social order 
that gave people a sense of 
what to expect from life.

The conclusion was startling: 
the likelihood of a person 
dying by suicide is significantly 
shaped not just by their 
individual psychology — 
but by the structure of 
the society they live in.

This does not mean individual 
psychology does not matter. 
It means it is not sufficient 
as an explanation.

---

**The two concepts that explain 
how society holds together — 
and how it breaks:**

Durkheim described two types of **solidarity** — 
the force that binds societies together.

**Mechanical solidarity** is the 
binding force in traditional, 
small-scale communities — 
where people are similar to each other, 
share the same beliefs, the same work, 
the same rituals, the same gods. 
Cohesion comes from sameness.

**Organic solidarity** is the binding 
force in complex modern societies — 
where people are highly different 
from each other, highly specialized, 
dependent on each other precisely 
because of their differences. 
The farmer, the doctor, the engineer, 
the teacher — none of them 
can survive alone. 
Cohesion comes from interdependence.

Modern societies function on 
organic solidarity. 
But it is fragile in ways that 
mechanical solidarity was not — 
because it depends on institutions, 
norms, and shared values 
that are not automatic. 
They must be built and maintained.

When they break down — 
when the interdependence becomes 
exploitation, when the shared values 
dissolve, when people no longer 
feel bound to or responsible for 
each other — Durkheim called this **anomie.**

**Anomie** is one of the most important 
words in sociology.

It means, literally, normlessness. 
A condition in which the social 
rules and expectations that give 
life structure and meaning 
have broken down.

Anomie does not feel like freedom. 
It feels like drift. 
Like the absence of anchoring. 
Like being surrounded by people 
without being connected to them.

Durkheim believed that anomie — 
not poverty alone, not individual 
weakness — was the primary 
social condition that drove 
suicide rates up.

---

### Applying Durkheim outside the West

Durkheim's concepts of solidarity 
and anomie are not only relevant 
to nineteenth-century France. 
They illuminate social dynamics 
across the world today.

Consider what has happened to 
communities across South Asia, 
Sub-Saharan Africa, and Latin America 
under the pressure of rapid 
urbanization and economic liberalization.

A farmer in rural India 
whose family has farmed the 
same land for generations 
is displaced by debt — 
partly produced by the costs 
of seeds and fertilizers from 
multinational agricultural companies. 
He moves to a city. 
He loses his land, his community, 
his role, his sense of what 
his life was for.

He has gained access to 
a cash economy. 
He has lost everything else 
that gave his life meaning and structure.

India has one of the highest 
farmer suicide rates in the world. 
Between 1995 and 2015, 
more than 300,000 Indian farmers 
died by suicide.

This is anomie — on a national scale. 
It is what happens when economic 
transformation destroys social solidarity 
faster than new forms of connection 
can be built.

The individual farmer's death 
is a personal tragedy. 
The pattern of 300,000 deaths 
is a social fact — 
produced by structural conditions 
that Durkheim would have 
recognized immediately.

---

### Apply it to your own life — Step by step

**Step 1:**
Think about a community you belong to 
or have belonged to — 
a family, a neighborhood, 
a religious community, a professional group.

**Step 2:**
Ask Durkheim's solidarity questions:
- What binds this community together?
- What shared beliefs, rituals, 
  obligations, or dependencies 
  make it a community 
  rather than just a group of individuals?
- What happens to members 
  who violate its norms?
- What happens to members 
  who leave?

**Step 3:**
Think of a time when that community's 
solidarity weakened or broke down. 
What happened? 
What did people lose 
beyond the material?

**Step 4:**
Now think about anomie in your 
own context.

Where in your society do you see 
people adrift — not just poor, 
not just unemployed, 
but disconnected from any 
meaningful social framework 
that gives their life structure?

What structural forces produced 
that disconnection?

**Step 5:**
Ask the policy question Durkheim implies:

If anomie is a structural condition — 
produced by the breakdown of solidarity — 
then what structural interventions 
could address it?

What would it mean to design 
a healthcare system, 
an education system, 
a technology platform, 
with solidarity rather than 
individual efficiency as its 
organizing principle?

## Thinker Three — Max Weber (1864–1920)

### Who he was

Max Weber was born in Erfurt, Germany, 
in 1864 into a family of educated, 
politically engaged Protestants.

His father was a lawyer and politician — 
authoritarian, pleasure-seeking, 
largely indifferent to his family's 
emotional needs. His mother was 
devout, ascetic, deeply ethical, 
and chronically miserable in her marriage.

Weber absorbed both of them 
and spent his life caught between 
their contradictions — 
between power and conscience, 
between worldly success and moral seriousness, 
between the demands of institutions 
and the call of individual conviction.

He was extraordinarily productive 
in his thirties — a professor at 
Freiburg and then Heidelberg, 
publishing major works across 
economics, history, religion, 
and sociology.

Then, in 1897, he had a severe 
breakdown. After a confrontation 
with his father — who died shortly 
after — Weber collapsed into 
what we would today recognize 
as severe depression and anxiety. 
He could not work, could not write, 
could barely function, 
for nearly five years.

He eventually recovered — 
partially, incompletely — 
and returned to produce some 
of the most important work 
in the history of social science.

His illness was not a footnote. 
It shaped his work profoundly. 
He understood from the inside 
what it means to be trapped — 
by expectation, by institution, 
by one's own internalized obligations — 
and unable to act according 
to one's own judgment.

The concept of the iron cage 
was not abstract for Max Weber.

---

### The world he was watching

Weber was writing during the consolidation 
of the modern state and the modern 
corporation — the emergence of 
large bureaucratic organizations 
that coordinated human activity 
at unprecedented scale.

He watched the Prussian state, 
the German military, the major 
corporations, the universities — 
all of them becoming more rational, 
more rule-bound, more formally organized.

This was, in many ways, an improvement 
over what preceded it. 
Decisions made by arbitrary 
personal authority, by tradition, 
by nepotism — these were being 
replaced by formal procedures, 
by trained expertise, by 
documented processes.

But Weber saw something that 
the enthusiasts of modernization missed.

As systems became more rational, 
more efficient, more formally organized — 
something was draining away from 
human life inside them.

The feeling of meaning. 
Of purpose. 
Of doing something because 
it was genuinely right, 
rather than because the 
procedure required it.

He called this **the disenchantment of the world** — 
the historical process by which 
magic, meaning, and mystery 
are stripped from human experience 
and replaced by calculation, 
procedure, and technical reason.

---

### His core tools — Power, authority, rationalization, and the iron cage

**Power and the three types of authority:**

Weber built on and complicated 
the understanding of power 
that Marx had established.

For Weber, power alone — 
the raw ability to force someone 
to do something — was sociologically 
less interesting than the question 
of **legitimacy.** Why do people obey?

He identified three types of authority — 
three different answers to that question.

**Traditional authority:** 
People obey because this is how 
it has always been done. 
The king rules because kings 
have always ruled. 
The elder is respected because 
elders have always been respected. 
The authority derives from 
the weight of custom and precedent.

**Charismatic authority:** 
People obey because they believe 
in the exceptional personal qualities 
of a specific individual — 
their vision, their courage, 
their connection to something divine 
or transcendent. 
The prophet. The revolutionary leader. 
The charismatic entrepreneur.

**Rational-legal authority:** 
People obey because the rules 
say they should — and the rules 
were made through legitimate procedures. 
The authority belongs to the office, 
not the person. 
A president commands because 
they hold the office of president — 
not because of their personal qualities 
or their family lineage.

Modern societies run primarily 
on rational-legal authority. 
This is generally more stable 
and fairer than the alternatives.

But Weber saw its hidden costs.

---

**Bureaucracy and the iron cage:**

Weber described bureaucracy with 
a precision that remains unsurpassed.

A bureaucracy, in Weber's analysis, 
is characterized by:
- A clear hierarchy of authority
- Rules and procedures that apply 
  universally, not personally
- Written documentation of everything
- Trained specialists who hold offices 
  based on qualification, not inheritance
- Separation of the office from 
  the person who holds it

This is how modern hospitals work. 
How government ministries work. 
How universities work. 
How corporations work. 
How AI systems are designed, 
deployed, and governed.

Bureaucracy is efficient. 
It treats similar cases similarly. 
It reduces arbitrary personal power.

But Weber saw the danger in it 
that was invisible to its enthusiasts.

As life becomes more bureaucratically 
organized, individual moral judgment 
gets replaced by procedural compliance. 
The question ceases to be 
*"is this right?"* 
and becomes 
*"does this comply with the regulations?"*

He described the result as 
**the iron cage** — 
a metaphor for the condition 
of modern people, locked inside 
systems of rational organization 
so comprehensive and so total 
that escape feels impossible 
and resistance feels irrational.

The person who follows orders 
because the procedure requires it, 
even when those orders cause harm — 
that is the iron cage in operation.

The doctor who cannot spend 
enough time with a patient 
because the system's efficiency 
metrics do not recognize 
the time that healing requires — 
that is the iron cage.

The AI ethics board that approves 
a harmful system because it 
technically meets the documented 
criteria — while knowing something 
is wrong — that is the iron cage.

---

**The concept of rationalization:**

Weber described a long historical 
process he called **rationalization** — 
the progressive application of 
formal reason, calculation, 
and systematic procedure 
to more and more areas of human life.

This process has produced 
enormous gains: modern medicine, 
modern science, modern law, 
modern infrastructure.

But it has also produced something 
Weber found deeply troubling: 
the erosion of the question 
*"what is this for?"*

A rationalized health system 
can be extraordinarily efficient 
at processing patients, 
minimizing costs, maximizing throughput — 
while losing the ability to ask 
whether efficiency is the right 
measure of healthcare at all.

A rationalized AI system 
can be extraordinarily good 
at optimizing for its defined metric — 
while that metric quietly embeds 
the inequalities of whoever defined it.

Weber's question is not 
"should we have rational systems?" 
His question is: 
*"Who decides what we are being 
rational toward — and whose interests 
does that serve?"*

---

### Applying Weber outside the West

Weber's analysis of bureaucracy 
and the iron cage is powerfully 
relevant to the experience of 
colonial and postcolonial institutions.

When colonial powers imposed 
bureaucratic administrative systems 
on India, Nigeria, Egypt, Pakistan — 
they brought with them 
the full apparatus of rational-legal authority: 
documented procedures, trained officials, 
formal hierarchies, written laws.

But these systems were designed 
to serve colonial extraction — 
not the welfare of the populations 
they governed.

The iron cage of colonial bureaucracy 
persisted long after independence 
in many countries — because the 
institutional structures remained 
even when the people in them changed.

A health ministry organized 
around the reporting requirements 
of international donors — 
measuring what donors want measured 
rather than what communities need — 
is a postcolonial iron cage.

It is rational. 
It is procedurally correct. 
It produces the required documentation.

And it consistently fails 
to serve the people it exists for — 
because its rationality was 
designed by and for someone else.

---

### Apply it to your own life — Step by step

**Step 1:**
Think of an institution you work in, 
study in, or interact with regularly. 
A hospital, a university, 
a government office, a company.

**Step 2:**
Ask Weber's authority question: 
Why do people obey the rules 
in this institution? 
Which type of authority is operating — 
traditional, charismatic, or rational-legal?

**Step 3:**
Now ask the iron cage question:

Is there a rule, procedure, 
or metric in this institution 
that everyone privately knows 
is not serving the actual purpose 
of the institution — 
but that everyone follows anyway 
because the system requires it?

What does it cost — 
in human terms — 
to follow that rule?

What does it cost — 
in personal and professional terms — 
to refuse to?

**Step 4:**
Ask Weber's deepest question:

What is this institution's 
official purpose? 
What is it actually optimizing for? 
Are those the same thing?

If they are not — 
who benefits from the gap?

## Thinker Four — Michel Foucault (1926–1984)

### Who he was

Michel Foucault was born in Poitiers, 
France in 1926, the son of a surgeon 
who expected his son to follow him 
into medicine.

He did not.

He studied philosophy and psychology 
in Paris after World War Two — 
a period of profound intellectual 
ferment, in which the experience 
of fascism and the Holocaust 
had forced European thinkers 
to ask radically new questions 
about power, knowledge, and 
the violence that rational, 
organized institutions were capable of.

Foucault was gay at a time when 
homosexuality was classified as 
a psychiatric disorder — 
in France, in the international 
medical literature, and in the 
social world he moved through. 
He was treated by the psychiatric 
institutions of his era not as 
a person with a different sexuality 
but as a person with a pathology 
to be corrected.

He had several severe depressive episodes. 
He attempted suicide as a young man. 
His father, the surgeon, responded 
by sending him for psychiatric treatment.

He came out of those experiences 
with a burning question that 
would define his entire intellectual life:

> *By what right does any institution — 
> a clinic, a prison, a school, 
> a hospital — claim the authority 
> to say what is normal, 
> what is deviant, 
> what is sane, 
> what is criminal, 
> what is healthy?*

He spent the next four decades 
trying to answer it.

He died in 1984 of AIDS-related illness — 
one of the early high-profile deaths 
in the epidemic. 
He was fifty-seven.

---

### The world he was watching

Foucault came of age in a Europe 
that had just watched rational, 
bureaucratic, scientifically sophisticated 
institutions — universities, hospitals, 
governments, militaries — 
organize and execute genocide.

The Holocaust was not carried out 
by irrational monsters. 
It was carried out by educated people, 
in institutions, following procedures, 
with documentation.

The medical profession participated 
directly — classifying, categorizing, 
selecting, experimenting.

Foucault's question was not 
"how did evil people gain power?" 
His question was: 
*"How do institutions that claim 
to be doing good — healing, educating, 
rehabilitating, reforming — 
produce harm? 
And how do they make that harm 
invisible — even to themselves?"*

He also lived through the 
psychiatric treatment of homosexuality — 
watching an institution that claimed 
to be healing people 
use its authority to enforce 
a social norm by calling it 
a medical fact.

The psychiatrist did not need 
to be cruel. 
The institution gave them 
a definition of normal, 
a set of procedures for treating 
deviation from that normal, 
and a genuine belief that 
they were helping.

That combination — 
sincere belief, institutional authority, 
and systematic harm — 
became the object of Foucault's life work.

---

### His core tools — Power/knowledge, discourse, and the disciplinary society

**Power/knowledge:**

Foucault's most radical contribution 
to sociology was the concept of 
**power/knowledge** — 
written with a slash because 
he insisted these two things 
cannot be separated.

Every claim to knowledge 
is also an exercise of power. 
The power to define what is true 
is the power to shape what is possible.

When a psychiatrist classifies 
a person as schizophrenic — 
that classification is simultaneously 
a knowledge claim (this is what 
this person has) and a power act 
(this is what will happen to this person 
as a result of this classification).

When a medical researcher defines 
"healthy weight" for a population — 
that definition is simultaneously 
a knowledge claim and a norm 
that will be used to evaluate, 
judge, and treat the bodies 
of millions of people.

When an AI system trained on 
historical healthcare data 
"learns" what a good health outcome 
looks like — it is encoding 
the power/knowledge of whoever 
defined those outcomes, 
in whatever populations they 
were measured in, 
under whatever conditions 
those studies were conducted.

Knowledge claims in medicine, 
in social science, in technology — 
are never neutral. 
They always carry with them 
the power of whoever produced them — 
and they exercise that power 
over everyone they are applied to.

---

**Discourse:**

Foucault used the term **discourse** 
to describe something more than 
just language or conversation.

A discourse is the entire framework — 
the concepts, categories, rules, 
assumptions, and institutions — 
within which a subject can be 
thought and spoken about at all.

The medical discourse does not just 
describe disease. 
It defines what counts as disease, 
who has the authority to make 
that definition, what follows 
from being classified as diseased, 
and what falls outside its frame entirely.

The discourse of mental health 
does not just describe psychological states. 
It defines what is sane and what is not, 
who is qualified to judge, 
what treatments are legitimate, 
and what experiences are pathological 
rather than, say, rational responses 
to irrational conditions.

To be inside a discourse is to 
speak its language so naturally 
that its assumptions become invisible. 
To be outside it is to find 
that your experience has no words — 
or that the available words 
systematically misrepresent 
what you are trying to say.

This is the experience of patients 
whose pain is described by doctors 
in terms that do not fit 
how they actually feel it.

This is the experience of communities 
whose health knowledge — 
built over generations of living 
in a specific place with specific 
conditions — is dismissed as 
anecdote by a medical system 
whose discourse has no category for it.

---

**The disciplinary society 
and the Panopticon:**

In his book *Discipline and Punish* (1975), 
Foucault analyzed how modern societies 
exercise power over individuals — 
not primarily through violence or force, 
but through **surveillance, normalization, and examination.**

He used the image of Jeremy Bentham's 
Panopticon — a prison design in which 
all cells surround a central tower 
from which a single guard can 
observe any prisoner at any moment — 
as a metaphor for modern power.

The genius of the Panopticon 
is that it does not require 
the guard to actually watch everyone. 
The prisoner does not know 
when they are being observed. 
So they must behave as if 
they are always being observed.

The watcher does not need to be present. 
The possibility of being watched 
is sufficient to produce 
self-regulation.

This is how modern institutions 
exercise power most efficiently:

Not by forcing people to comply — 
but by making them internalize 
the standards they are expected to meet, 
and monitor themselves accordingly.

The student who studies 
not from curiosity but from 
fear of the examination — 
that is the Panopticon.

The patient who describes their symptoms 
in the language the doctor expects, 
editing out anything that 
seems "irrational" or "irrelevant" — 
that is the Panopticon.

The researcher who frames their findings 
in ways that will satisfy 
the funding body's priorities — 
that is the Panopticon.

The AI that is trained on 
what gets measured and rewarded 
by the institution that built it — 
and learns to optimize for 
that measurement regardless 
of whether it serves 
actual human wellbeing — 
that is the Panopticon.

---

### Applying Foucault to health and AI

Foucault is perhaps the most directly 
useful thinker for understanding 
healthcare AI — not despite being 
a philosopher of the nineteenth and 
twentieth centuries, but because 
of what he saw so clearly about 
institutions and power.

**On clinical AI:**

A clinical AI system that predicts 
patient risk is a power/knowledge 
apparatus in Foucault's sense.

It claims knowledge: 
this patient is high risk, 
low risk, likely to comply, 
likely to deteriorate.

That knowledge claim immediately 
becomes a power act: 
resources are allocated or withheld, 
interventions are offered or not offered, 
attention is directed here and away from there.

The patient did not choose 
to be classified by this system. 
They often do not know it exists. 
They cannot interrogate its criteria. 
They cannot appeal its decisions.

The AI is the Panopticon's 
logical evolution — 
watching, classifying, 
normalizing at scale, 
without any single watcher 
being responsible for 
any particular decision.

**On the definition of normal:**

Who decided what a normal 
blood pressure looks like? 
What a normal body weight is? 
What a normal level of pain 
is acceptable?

These definitions were not 
discovered in nature. 
They were constructed — 
by researchers, in studies, 
with specific populations, 
at specific historical moments, 
under specific institutional pressures.

When those definitions are 
built into AI systems 
that are then deployed globally — 
they carry the power/knowledge 
of whoever defined "normal" 
into every clinical encounter 
those systems touch.

---

### Apply it to your own life — Step by step

**Step 1:**
Think of a time when you were 
formally evaluated by an institution. 
A medical examination. 
A school assessment. 
A job performance review. 
A psychiatric or psychological assessment.

**Step 2:**
Ask the Foucauldian questions:

- Who had the power to define 
  what a good outcome looked like 
  in that evaluation?
- What was the standard you were 
  being measured against, 
  and who decided that standard?
- Whose body, whose mind, 
  whose behavior was the implicit 
  "normal" in that evaluation?
- What happened to you as a result 
  of that classification? 
  What became possible — 
  and what became closed off?

**Step 3:**
Now ask the surveillance question:

Before that evaluation happened — 
did you change your behavior 
because you knew it was coming? 
Did you edit what you said, 
how you presented yourself, 
what you admitted?

That editing is the Panopticon working. 
The institution did not need 
to be present in the room 
for its power to shape your behavior. 
You carried it with you.

**Step 4:**
Ask the discourse question:

Was there something true about 
your experience that the 
evaluation's framework 
had no category for? 
Something you could not say 
because the available language 
would have distorted it?

That gap — between your experience 
and the institution's ability to see it — 
is the gap between lived reality 
and the discourse that claims 
to represent it.

It is where the most important 
sociological work happens.

## Thinker Five — Pierre Bourdieu (1930–2002)

### Who he was

Pierre Bourdieu was born in a tiny village 
called Denguin, in the Béarn region 
of southwestern France, 
in 1930. His father was a postman.

He was the first person in his family 
to attend university.

He went on to become the most 
cited sociologist of the 
twentieth century.

Between those two facts 
is a lifetime of crossing — 
and the theory that crossing 
produced.

Bourdieu was brilliant enough 
to move from a rural, working-class 
world into the most elite 
institutions of French intellectual life. 
He attended the École Normale Supérieure — 
the most prestigious academic 
institution in France — 
alongside people who had been 
preparing for that institution 
since childhood.

He never forgot the violence 
of that crossing. 
Not physical violence — 
but the quiet, constant experience 
of being in rooms where everything — 
the references people made, 
the way they dressed, 
the confidence with which they spoke, 
the assumptions they took for granted — 
marked him as someone from elsewhere.

He called this experience 
**symbolic violence** — 
the harm done not by force 
but by the systematic devaluation 
of who you are, 
what you know, 
and where you come from.

He spent his career trying to 
explain, with scientific precision, 
how inequality reproduces itself 
not just through money — 
but through culture, taste, 
language, and the invisible 
marks that social class 
inscribes on people's bodies 
before they have a chance 
to make a single choice.

He remained publicly active 
until the end of his life — 
marching with striking workers, 
writing against neoliberal 
economic policies, refusing 
the comfortable position 
of the detached intellectual.

He died of cancer in 2002. 
He was seventy-one.

---

### The world he was watching

Bourdieu was writing in postwar France — 
a society that believed, 
with considerable genuine conviction, 
that it had built a meritocracy.

Education was the mechanism of that belief. 
The French educational system 
was formally open to everyone. 
The same examinations. 
The same curriculum. 
The same credential at the end.

But Bourdieu looked at the data 
and found something that the 
official story could not explain:

The most prestigious schools — 
the grandes écoles that trained 
France's political, economic, 
and intellectual elite — 
were overwhelmingly populated 
by the children of people 
who had already attended 
those schools.

The meritocracy was producing 
the same elites, generation 
after generation, through a system 
that looked formally equal 
but was structurally tilted.

The question was: how? 

If the examinations were the same, 
the curriculum was the same, 
the credential was the same — 
what was producing these 
consistent, predictable outcomes?

His answer: 
the competition was never equal, 
because the contestants 
did not arrive equally prepared. 
And the preparation that mattered 
most was invisible to the 
official account of merit.

---

### His core tools — Capital, field, and habitus

**Capital:**

Bourdieu expanded Marx's concept 
of capital beyond the economic.

**Economic capital** is money, 
assets, property — 
the ability to purchase things, 
including access to education, 
healthcare, legal representation, 
and safety.

**Cultural capital** is knowledge, 
skills, tastes, credentials, 
and ways of being 
that are recognized and valued 
by dominant institutions.

Cultural capital comes in three forms:

*Embodied cultural capital* — 
dispositions, ways of speaking, 
confidence, physical comportment, 
taste in art, literature, food — 
absorbed through upbringing, 
not purchased. 
You cannot buy the ease 
with which a privately educated child 
addresses a teacher as an equal. 
It was given to them 
through years of being treated 
as a person whose questions matter.

*Objectified cultural capital* — 
books, art, instruments, tools — 
things that must be purchased 
but that only provide advantage 
when combined with the 
embodied knowledge to use them.

*Institutionalized cultural capital* — 
formal credentials: degrees, 
certifications, licenses. 
The official recognition 
by an institution that you 
possess a certain level 
of knowledge or skill.

**Social capital** is who you know — 
the networks, connections, 
relationships, and obligations 
that provide access to 
resources, information, 
and opportunities.

The child of a doctor who 
wants to become a doctor 
does not just inherit economic resources. 
They inherit the embodied confidence 
to perform well in medical school interviews. 
They inherit social capital — 
the ability to call a family friend 
who works in a hospital 
for advice, access, recommendation. 
They inherit cultural capital — 
years of medical vocabulary 
absorbed around the dinner table, 
a feel for what medicine values, 
how it talks about itself.

None of this is visible 
in the examination room. 
All of it shapes the outcome.

---

**Field:**

Bourdieu described social life 
as organized into **fields** — 
relatively autonomous arenas 
of social practice, 
each with its own rules, 
its own forms of capital, 
its own stakes.

Medicine is a field. 
Academia is a field. 
Law is a field. 
The art world is a field. 
Technology is a field.

Within each field, people compete 
for position using the forms 
of capital that field values.

But — and this is crucial — 
each field defines which capital counts.

The medical field values certain 
credentials, certain ways of knowing, 
certain research methods, 
certain institutional affiliations.

Someone who brings deep community-based 
knowledge of how a disease 
affects a specific population — 
knowledge built over years 
of living with those people, 
in that place — 
may not have the capital 
the medical field recognizes.

Their knowledge is real. 
Their capital is simply 
the wrong kind.

This is not a neutral fact. 
It is a structural feature 
of how fields protect themselves 
and reproduce their existing hierarchies.

---

**Habitus:**

Habitus is Bourdieu's most 
profound and most misunderstood concept.

It is the set of durable dispositions — 
ways of perceiving, thinking, feeling, 
and acting — that are acquired 
through experience in particular 
social positions, 
and that generate practices 
without requiring conscious thought.

Habitus is history made body.

The child who grows up in poverty 
does not consciously decide 
to lower their expectations. 
Their habitus — formed through 
years of experiences in which 
certain things were simply 
not available to people like them — 
makes certain possibilities 
feel unreal, inappropriate, 
not for them.

The child who grows up in privilege 
does not consciously decide 
to feel entitled to be heard. 
Their habitus — formed through 
years of being listened to, 
taken seriously, treated as 
someone whose opinions matter — 
produces an ease in institutional 
settings that they experience 
as simply their personality.

Both are products of structure. 
Both feel entirely natural 
to the people who have them.

Habitus is why structural inequality 
reproduces itself even in the 
absence of overt discrimination. 
People from disadvantaged positions 
often do not apply for things 
they could qualify for — 
because their habitus tells them 
those things are not for them. 
People from privileged positions 
often succeed in competitions 
they are no more qualified for — 
because their habitus tells them 
to try, to expect, to belong.

---

**Symbolic violence:**

Bourdieu's final key concept 
is **symbolic violence** — 
a form of power that operates 
through the imposition of 
categories of perception and 
evaluation that serve 
dominant interests, 
on dominated people, 
with their own complicity.

Symbolic violence is the harm 
of being made to see yourself, 
your community, your knowledge, 
your way of being in the world — 
through the evaluative framework 
of a system that was not 
built with you in mind.

When a brilliant student 
from a rural, working-class community 
sits in a university classroom 
and feels — despite their ability — 
that they do not quite belong, 
that they are slightly wrong 
in a way they cannot articulate — 
that is symbolic violence.

When a patient whose healing 
practices come from a tradition 
outside Western biomedicine 
is told by a doctor that 
what they believe about their 
own body is not real — 
that is symbolic violence.

When a community's health knowledge — 
built over generations of living 
in proximity to a disease — 
is dismissed as anecdote 
because it is not formatted 
as a randomized controlled trial — 
that is symbolic violence.

The violence is real. 
The weapon is classification.

---

### Applying Bourdieu to global health and AI

Bourdieu's concepts of capital, field, 
and habitus illuminate something 
crucial about how health systems 
and AI systems are built.

**On who gets to build:**

The field of health AI is dominated 
by people with specific forms 
of capital — degrees from specific 
universities, access to specific 
research infrastructure, 
fluency in specific technical languages.

This is not simply the result 
of merit. 
It is the result of who 
had access to the educational 
and social capital that 
this field recognizes as legitimate.

The communities whose health 
is being modeled by these systems — 
who often have deep knowledge 
of their own health conditions, 
their own risk factors, 
their own needs — 
rarely hold the capital 
the field recognizes.

Their knowledge is excluded 
not because it is wrong — 
but because it is the wrong kind 
of capital.

**On whose habitus is built in:**

The datasets that train clinical AI 
systems reflect the habitus 
of the research institutions 
that produced them — 
what they measured, who they 
included, what they defined 
as a relevant variable, 
what they left out.

When that habitus is largely 
that of wealthy, Western, 
predominantly male research institutions — 
the AI system that learns from it 
will perform best on people 
whose bodies, behaviors, and 
health trajectories resemble 
those who were included.

Everyone else becomes an edge case — 
not because they are rare, 
but because their reality was 
not capitalized in the field 
that built the system.

---

### Apply it to your own life — Step by step

**Step 1:**
Think about the forms of capital 
you carry into your professional 
or academic field.

What economic capital 
supported your education? 
What cultural capital — 
knowledge, credentials, 
ways of speaking and presenting — 
do you bring? 
What social capital — 
networks, connections, 
people who will vouch for you — 
are available to you?

**Step 2:**
Now think about what capital 
you lack that your field values.

Where do you feel the gap? 
What rooms do you enter 
where your capital is not 
the right kind?

**Step 3:**
Try to identify your habitus 
operating in a specific moment.

Think of a recent situation 
where you either:
- Did not try for something 
  you were qualified for, 
  because some part of you 
  felt it was not for you
- Attempted something with 
  a confidence that surprised 
  people around you — 
  a confidence that came not 
  from calculation but from 
  a deep sense that you belonged

In both cases — 
trace that response back. 
Where did it come from? 
What structural position 
produced that habitus?

**Step 4:**
The hardest question Bourdieu asks:

What do you know — 
from your experience, 
your community, 
your specific social position — 
that your professional field 
does not recognize as valid knowledge?

What would it mean for your field 
if it did?

## The Five Tools Together

You now have five tools.

Before you move to Level 3, 
you need to be able to hold 
more than one at the same time.

The world does not present itself 
in neat theoretical boxes. 
The mother waiting in the hospital 
is being shaped by all five 
simultaneously:

By **Marx** — 
she is in a structural position 
determined by her class relationship 
to the means of production. 
Her ability to access care 
is conditioned by economic capital 
she does not have.

By **Durkheim** — 
her experience of illness 
is shaped by the degree of 
social solidarity in her community, 
the presence or absence of 
social support networks, 
the collective meaning she can 
draw on to make sense of 
suffering.

By **Weber** — 
the hospital she enters 
is a bureaucratic institution 
organized around rational-legal authority, 
optimizing for metrics that 
may or may not correspond 
to what she actually needs. 
She interacts with it as a 
subject of its rational-legal order — 
not as a person whose judgment 
carries equal weight.

By **Foucault** — 
the moment she enters the clinic, 
she enters a discourse that 
will define her condition 
in terms she did not create, 
apply a standard of normal 
she was not consulted about, 
and produce knowledge about her 
that will shape what happens to her 
without her having meaningful 
power over it.

By **Bourdieu** — 
she enters the medical field 
with a specific habitus, 
a specific set of capitals, 
a specific sense of what 
she is entitled to ask for, 
how loudly, in what language. 
Her sense of what is possible 
in that room was formed 
long before she arrived in it.

---

This is what it means to think 
sociologically. 
Not to pick the right theory 
and apply it. 
But to hold multiple lenses 
simultaneously — 
and to see, in any specific situation, 
the full complexity of the 
structural forces at work.

### Exercise 1 — The multi-lens analysis

Read the following scenario. 
Then analyze it using all five thinkers. 
Write one paragraph for each.

**Scenario:**

A hospital in a major city in 
sub-Saharan Africa implements 
a new AI-powered triage system — 
built by a European technology company 
using data from European hospitals. 
The system is designed to prioritize 
patients for immediate care 
based on predicted risk.

Within months, nurses notice 
that the system consistently 
deprioritizes elderly patients 
and patients who speak local languages 
rather than the official national language. 
When they raise concerns, 
they are told the system 
has been validated and 
meets international standards.

---

Apply each thinker:

**Marx:** 
Who owns this system? 
Who profits from its deployment? 
Whose labor — the nurses, 
the patients, the data workers — 
produced the value it extracts? 
What ideology makes its authority 
feel legitimate?

**Durkheim:** 
What happens to the solidarity 
of the medical community 
when nurses' experiential knowledge 
is consistently overridden 
by a system that does not 
speak their language or 
know their community? 
What form of anomie is 
being produced here?

**Weber:** 
The hospital adopted this system 
because it "meets international standards." 
This is rational-legal authority 
operating. 
What is the iron cage 
in this scenario? 
Who is trapped inside it — 
and what does compliance cost them?

**Foucault:** 
The system defines triage priority — 
which is simultaneously a knowledge claim 
(this patient is lower risk) 
and a power act (this patient waits). 
Who has the power to challenge 
that classification? 
What discourse makes the system's 
authority legible — 
and what discourse makes 
the nurses' knowledge illegible?

**Bourdieu:** 
What capitals does the technology company 
bring to this field? 
What capitals do the nurses bring? 
Whose capital is recognized 
by the institution? 
What is the symbolic violence 
in being told your direct 
clinical experience is less valid 
than a validated algorithm?

## A Note on Limitations

These five thinkers are all European. 
Four of them are men. 
All of them wrote primarily 
about European or North American societies.

This is not a coincidence. 
It is the result of which intellectual 
traditions were given resources, 
institutional support, and global circulation — 
and which were not.

As you move into Level 3 
and build your own research agenda, 
you will encounter thinkers 
whose tools are equally powerful 
and far less familiar to 
the Western sociological canon:

W.E.B. Du Bois — 
whose concept of the color line 
and double consciousness 
predates and in many ways exceeds 
Weber's analysis of power and identity.

Frantz Fanon — 
whose analysis of colonial violence 
and its psychological consequences 
is essential for any sociologist 
working in postcolonial contexts.

Patricia Hill Collins — 
whose concept of intersectionality 
(developed alongside Kimberlé Crenshaw) 
addresses what happens when 
multiple structures — race, class, 
gender, disability — operate 
simultaneously on the same person.

B.R. Ambedkar — 
whose analysis of caste as 
a social structure of exclusion 
offers tools for understanding 
inequality that the European 
canon entirely lacks.

These thinkers are not supplements 
to the classical tradition. 
They are corrections to it — 
and in some cases, improvements on it.

They will appear throughout 
the Level 3 field modules 
and beyond.
